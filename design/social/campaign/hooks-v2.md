## 1. Verdict in five bullets

1. **The owner is right on both counts.** The rejected slogan is a policy statement, not a promise; the
   hook “your street has rules now” is a positioning sentence doing a hook’s job badly; and the five
   on-screen lines explain the product to someone who has not yet been given a reason to care.
2. **The real problem is that the copy never personifies the map.** The video is now dots, rings and a
   clock — the strongest thing in the product (“those dots are my friends, hiding from me”) is exactly
   what none of the current lines say. The map does the visual work; the copy must do the human work.
3. **The rejected v1 line answered objections nobody had yet.** At second 12 the viewer
   is not thinking “will there be equipment?” — they are thinking “wait, *what* is this?” A line made of
   two absences and one generic noun cannot survive being forwarded to a group chat.
4. **A hook here has one job: make a stranger ask a question they want answered by the map.** Not explain
   fairness, not state the category, not list rules. Curiosity + a human stake, in ≤ 5 words, works muted.
5. **My independent recommendation:** primary line **“Nobody plays alone.”**; first-1.5s hook
   **“every dot is a friend.”**; sharp social cut **“No solo mode.”**; functional three-beat
   **“Hide. Track. Tag.”** — a system that beats the rejected line on curiosity, shareability, growth
   logic and honesty, and is compatible with the brand-director’s sport/chalk visual identity if they
   keep it. Details in §6.

## 2. Blunt critique of the current lines

### 2.1 “your street has rules now” — why it does not stop a scroll

| Problem | What happens in the viewer’s head |
|---|---|
| **It is a positioning line, not a hook** | “Rules” is an administrative abstraction. There is no person, no action, no image, no question. A viewer can’t replay a rule in their head; they can replay a dot moving. |
| **“Your street” assumes a life** | Most viewers are inside when they see it. The line presumes ownership of a street and a habit of being on it; for the actual target (friend groups on a couch deciding what to do tonight) it is aspirational in the wrong direction. |
| **“now” is fake novelty** | It implies a before/after the video never shows. Compare “now” with a line that shows the change: “hiding stopped working.” |
| **The grammar is inert** | A noun and a time adverb. No second-person verb (“run”, “hide”, “send”), no imperative, no question, no conflict. Nothing happens. |
| **It cannot be shown** | The video can show a dot, a ring, a trail, a clock. It cannot show “rules.” The strongest word on screen is the least filmable. |
| **It arrives before the context** | In 1.5 s the viewer is still identifying the visual system (map? game? app?). Asking them to process a brand-positioning claim at that moment burns the only attention window the edit has. |
| **It is not quotable** | Nobody forwards this to a friend to say “this is us.” A shareable hook must be repeatable in a group chat: “your phone is the snitch” is; “your street has rules now” is not. |
| **What is right about it** | The lowercase chalk register, the night-street mood, and its brevity. Keep the *tone*; change the *job*. |

**Verdict:** it is a good line for a poster on a wall where people already know the game. It is a weak
line for a feed where nobody does.

### 2.2 The five on-screen lines as a set

| Current line | What it is doing | What the viewer experiences | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| `two teams` | Structural label | “Okay… so?” | Not wrong, but it is a fact, not a stake. Works only once the viewer already wants to play. |
| `one clock` | Feature label | “Most games have a clock.” | Dead on arrival — the clock means nothing until somebody might lose because of it. |
| `the map already knows` | The only line doing a hook’s work | A flicker of intrigue, then “knows what?” — and it lands at ~7 s, far too late | The right instinct, delayed to the wrong beat and shadowed by surveillance associations. |
| `tagged` | Payoff label | “Tagged… who? I never met anyone in this video.” | A payoff with no protagonist. In a map-only edit the viewer has no person to root for, so the tag has no weight. |
| end card | Slogan + CTA + honest install line | Correctly informative | The rejected slogan is the problem here; the structure (install line, “not on Play Store”, trust line) is right and should stay. |

**Systemic diagnosis (why the set feels flat):**

1. **Explanation instead of desire.** Every line tells the viewer what the product is; none gives them
   something they want to find out. Explanation belongs in the caption or the comments.
2. **No protagonist.** The dots are people, but the copy never says so. Without “you”, “your friends”, or
   “they”, the video is a systems demo with music.
3. **No escalation.** Five flat declaratives at equal weight. Tension needs a setup (“they’re hiding”) and
   a payoff (“the map shows where”). The current order has neither; it is a feature carousel with a clock.
4. **No address.** The viewer is never spoken to or placed in the scene. The most magnetic words in this
   product are “you” and “your friends”, and they are absent for the first ten seconds.
5. **No quotable line.** Shares require a phrase worth repeating to the group chat. Nothing here survives
   being said out loud by a stranger.
6. **Wrong sequence for a scroll.** Brand lockup first, intrigue last. A feed video has to earn the logo,
   not open with it: intrigue → mechanic → payoff → identity + address.
7. **The map-only revision makes this worse unless copy compensates.** A human face can carry a flat line;
   an animated map cannot. With no people on screen, the copy is the only place to put the people.
8. **The guardrails were applied to attention instead of claims.** The brand guardrails are about not
   promising safety, not inventing users, not implying Play Store — not about being interesting. “Safe”
   copy has been confused with *safe content*.

**What to keep:** the lowercase chalk caption voice, the animated map, the cyan field / coral Prey /
violet Hunters semantics, the wordmark bug, the honest end-card install line, the OSM credit. The visual
system is not the problem. The words are.

### 2.3 Why the rejected v1 line deserved rejection (historical)

- **It answered a question the viewer hadn’t asked.** At first contact the question is “what is this?”
  not “do I need equipment?” Gear and fee are *later* objections (install-page, comment replies).
- **Two of its three beats were absences.** A policy statement with one generic noun; the only warm word
  could sell a gym membership, a five-a-side app or a board-game café.
- **It encoded no hiding, no map, no chase.** It could belong to any pickup activity. That is fatal for
  a product whose whole novelty is “the hiding has a tracker.”
- **It attracted the wrong intent.** Race-to-zero framing pulls freebie-seekers with low conversion into a
  sideload; the brand’s stronger pull is social (“bring your friends”), not cheap.
- **It was defensive at the moment of maximum attention.** The cheapest place to say “free, not Play
  Store, 2 MB” is the end card’s install line — where it already exists and is already honest.
- **Brand-director was right about the underlying strategy** (group, sport frame, no safety promises) and
  wrong about the words. The sport frame can survive without a slogan about cost.

## 3. What the revised video actually needs

**Constraints carried from the motion workstream:** captions bottom-left, Big Shoulders, two lines max
at ~12 characters per line; bottom ~320 px kept clear; lowercase chalk tone; lockup only on the end card;
no fixed clock claim (host sets the length); no real people; no safety promises; OSM credit if tiles show.

**The five hook tests** (a line must pass all five to be recommended):

1. **Person test** — does it imply a human (you, your friends, them), not just a system?
2. **Question test** — does it create a question the viewer needs the next 3 seconds to answer?
3. **Out-loud test** — would a 20-year-old say it to a friend in a group chat?
4. **Mute test** — readable at a glance, at 1x phone size, with no narration?
5. **Claim test** — is every word defensible from the repo (no safety, no fake counts, no fixed length)?

**Recommended copy order for a 15 s map-only edit (timings indicative — fit the motion edit):**

| Beat | Job | Recommended line (or A/B) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0–1.5 s | Make a person appear in a dot | **every dot is a friend.** |
| 1.5–4 s | Say who they are | **they’re hiding right now.** |
| 4.0–7.0 s | The asymmetry | **one team sees the dots.** |
| 7.0–10.0 s | The rule that ends it | **one touch and you’re tagged.** |
| 10.0–12.0 s | Why it’s fair / why it’s a game | **the clock is the server’s.** |
| 12.0–15.0 s | End card: identity + address + install | **Nobody plays alone.** + free APK · Android 8+ · not on Play Store · 74.208.99.178 · “Play with people you trust.” |

Alternates if the motion-designer wants a different first beat: `one team sees the dots.` (tension-first)
or `your friends are hiding.` (relationship-first). All three fit two lines on the existing caption band.

## 5. Top three copy directions

### Direction A — “The Map Is Alive” (recommended)
**Hooks:** 11, 12, 14, 13, 15. **Idea:** the map is not a UI, it is a room full of people you know. Copy
personifies the dots and lets the viewer’s own friend group fill in the faces. Tone: mysterious, warm,
quiet — the voice of someone leaning over your shoulder at night.
**Why it wins:** it is the only direction that fully uses the map-only edit; it is impossible to confuse
with a generic app ad; it creates a question in four words; Arabic is clean and colloquial.
**Risk:** without the personification line, the map stays abstract. Needs discipline: one human word per
beat (“friend”, “hiding”, “they”).

### Direction B — “The Group Dare”
**Hooks:** 06, 07, 08, 09, 10. **Idea:** the video is a dare to assemble your people; the product truth
(no solo) becomes the challenge. Tone: competitive, playful, group-chat energy.
**Why it wins:** directly drives the only conversion that matters for a 2+ phone game — the group-chat
send; hooks double as CTAs; comment-bait is honest (“who’s your first hide?”).
**Risk:** can tip into engagement-bait; must stay a real requirement, never a fake giveaway; weak alone on
the end card.

### Direction C — “The Rules of the Hunt”
**Hooks:** 01, 02, 03, 04, 05. **Idea:** the game has rules you can’t bend — phone reports, server keeps
time, one touch ends it. Tone: clipped, sport-broadcast, fair.
**Why it wins:** credibility for the Android/dev crowd; explains the product in the same breath as the
drama; excellent for beats 2–5 of the video and for community posts.
**Risk:** cold as a first beat — a rule is not a reason to watch. Best used *behind* a personified hook.

**Recommended mix for the revised short:** A for the first 1.5 s, C for the middle beats, B for the end
card and CTA. That is the minimum-change option: the visual edit stays, the words do the work.

## 8. Arabic adaptation notes

- **Register:** MSA on the install page and app; Egyptian colloquial on TikTok/Reels; never mix within
  one asset. Use the app’s established terms: مطاردة (match), غرفة (room), فريسة (Prey), وسم (tag);
  in external copy prefer المطاردون (the chasers) over الصيادون.
- **Hook length:** Arabic glyphs are wider — keep hooks to **2 beats, ≤ 4 words each**, and re-check the
  Big Shoulders/IBM Plex Sans Arabic line at 390 px. If it does not fit, cut a beat; never shrink type.
- **Latin words stay isolated:** ManHunt, Android, APK, Google Play on their own line or with spaces;
  Western digits only; never mirror the clock/timer.
- **Don’t transliterate the slogan.** Use the Arabic renderings in §6.3 / §7; they are adaptations of
  meaning, not translations of words.
- **Native review required** for every Arabic string before posting; the bar is “reads like Arabic”,
  not “matches the English”.
